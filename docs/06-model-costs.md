Each success rate comes with a 90% Clopper-Pearson interval: with four or five problems per level the interval is enormous, and stating it is the only honest way to give the number.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| easy | 4 | 4 | 100% | 47% – 100% | $0.0674 | — |
| medium | 3 | 2 | 67% | 14% – 98% | $0.1150 | $0.6886 |
| hard | 4 | 3 | 75% | 25% – 99% | $0.1822 | $1.2486 |
| **total** | 11 | 9 | 82% | 53% – 97% | $0.1162 | $0.9686 |

**MEASURED** — the four `easy` entries are problems of categoria `test`, cioe' controlli di sanita' scritti dagli autori the archive: 4 out of 4. The seven `medium` and `hard` entries are variants di congetture vere e proprie, di categoria `research solved`: 5 su 7. The number to remember is **5 out of 7**, not 9 out of 11.

**MEASURED** — mean cost of a success $0.1162; mean cost of a failure $0.9686. A failure costs 8 times a success, because a failure consumes the whole per-problem cap while a success stops as soon as dimostrazione passa.

**MEASURED** — cost of the FIRST iteration, median over 11 problems: $0.0430. Two of the nine successes arrived on the first iteration. This is the price of a single shot, and it is the basis of strategia mista del punto 4.

**MISURATO** — tempo di calendario: 0.45 hours in tutto, di cui 0.21 hours di Lean in local (46%). The bottleneck is not the API: it is the verifier.

## 4. Proiezione sui problems open_problems

The probabilities of success on an **open** problem are not measurable: they are the three hypotheses below, and every line declares what it rests on. The arithmetic accounts for the targets being **finite** in number: once a strategy has exhausted them, extra spending buys nothing this model can evaluate.

### Cost of an attempt (the basis of the arithmetic)

- **A, full attempt** on an open statement: **$0.97** — **MEASURED**, the mean of the calibration's two failures ($0.6886 fermato dalle 20 iterations, $1.2486 stopped by the spending cap). On an open problem an attempt finisce quasi sempre cosi'.
- **C, single shot** (the sieve): **$0.0430** — **MEASURED**, the median cost of the first iteration over the 11 calibration problems.
- **B, program di ricerca** scritto e lanciato: **$0.10** di API per problem — **ESTIMATED**, of the order of the measured cost of the easy facili (media $0.0674). Local computation costs no dollars: it costs machine-nights.

I take about a fifth of that ceiling, because the probe tries tactics whereas the agent reasons — so it can do better — but 9.5% is the ceiling of a sample of 30, not an estimate.

- **pessimistic: p = 0.02%** — STIMATO. one success every 5000: the archive is curated by DeepMind to collect problems on which the experts have stopped.

### Strategy B — hunting counterexamples with local computation

Here the limit is not money: it is the number of problems on which a search makes sense. **MEASURED**: the selection script found 30 of them suitable for computation across the whole archive. Of the four whose literature I checked, two have a reachable frontier (numbers di Euclide: nessuna ricerca sistematica pubblicata; congettura di Selfridge: verificata only fino a k circa 29) e two no (Erdos 366: verificata fino a 10^22; Goldbach e Legendre: fino a 4x10^18). Due su quattro, interval di Clopper-Pearson al 90% 10% – 90%: **ESTIMATED** 50%, so about **15 targets real_list**.

## 6. What cannot be estimated

1. **The probability that an open problem is solvable by this system.** It is the number that decides everything, and it is exactly the one I do not have. No sample of solved open problems exists on which to measure it: if it did, those problems would not be open. The three scenarios in section 4 are my hypotheses, not measurements.

2. **Why calibrating on solved problems is optimistic.** A problem with its proof in the archive has, by construction, a short proof: the eleven used here run from 1 to 34 lines. Whoever wrote the statement already knew it closed, and formalised it so that it would. On an open problem there is no guarantee that a short proof exists, nor that the statement is phrased in an attackable form. The measured 71% (5 of 7) is the rate on a population that contains NO open problem.

3. **Memorisation.** I chose problems that entered the archive after the declared training cutoff, but the cutoff concerns the archive, not the mathematics: Fermat's quadruple and the 17-vertex counterexample have been in the literature for decades. How much of the 9 successes is reconstruction and how much is recall, I cannot measure.

4. **How much the iteration limit matters.** One of the two failures stopped because it ran out of its 20 iterations, not because it could not do it: with 60 iterations it might have closed. I have not tried, so I do not count it.

5. **The value of a finding.** If a search finds a counterexample, the protocol in `docs/04-finding-protocol.md` foresees three outcomes: faulty formalisation, already-known result, new candidate. With zero findings so far I have no data on how they would divide, and the likeliest case a priori is the first.

## 7. Raccomandazione

**The mixed strategy (C), and below $174 there is no reason di fare other.** Tre ragioni, in order di weight.

1. *The sieve costs almost nothing and covers everything.* A single shot at one problem costs $0.0430, **MEASURED**. With **$50** you get 1163 single shots: more than the archive's 1241 verifiable open problems. That is, fifty dollars tries EVERY open problem in the collection once, and shows where the model has a plan and where it does not. No other spending in this project has a comparable informazione/prezzo simile.

2. *Full attempts should be bought afterwards, not before.* An attempt costs $0.97, **MEASURED**, and ends unsolved almost every time. Buying one for each of the 1241 open problems costs $1 202 and is the worst way to spend it. Sieving all of them and attacking the best 124 costs $174 and, in the realistic scenario, returns **2.9 times** the successes per dollar of strategy A.

3. *Local computation is free: always saturate it.* Hunting counterexamples consumes no API budget, only machine-nights. It should be kept running alongside any strategy, because its marginal cost in dollars is zero. But it has to be aimed at the few problems where the published bounds are low: where the literature has reached 10^22, no night of computation changes anything.

**At what level of spending it makes sense to attempt the open problems.**

- **$53** — the complete sieve: one shot at all 1241 open_problems verificabili. Successi expected 5.4 / 0.813 / 0.054 (ottimistico / realistico / pessimistico). Ha senso comunque, also expecting zero successes: what you buy is the map of where the model has a plan.
- **$174** — the complete sieve plus full attempts on the best 10%. Expected successes 13 / 1.6 / 0.080. This is the point where, if the realistic scenario is right, a success becomes more likely than not. Below it there is no reason to do anything else; above it, you are betting on a number nobody knows.
- **$500** — expected successes 20 / 2.6 / 0.147. Worth it only if the setaccio da $53 ha mostrato targets promettenti: spent alla cieca, it buys attempts on problems where the model had no plan at all.
- **$1000–$5000** — expected successes 30 / 4.1 / 0.250 and 35 / 4.9 / 0.303. Past saturation the arithmetic loses meaning: it would buy second and third attempts at the same problems, and the model treats them as independent of the first, which they are not. I would not recommend it without reading the sieve's data first.

Note the width: at every level of spending the three scenarios span two orders of magnitude. **The uncertainty is not in the arithmetic: it is all in the value of p**, which section 6 declares unestimable. Anyone giving a single number here is guessing.

**A recommendation about targets, not only about spending.** The 1550 problems marcati `research solved` but lacking a proof in the archive are an intermediate class: the mathematics is known, the formalisation is missing. On those the success rate would be REAL (the outcome can be checked), the result is useful to the archive, and the risk of spending for nothing is far lower. If the goal is 'do useful mathematical work with this system' rather than 'solve an open problem', that is the route with the best ratio of cost to result — and the calibration in hand describes it better than it describes the open problems.
