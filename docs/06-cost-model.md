## 2. The model, level by level

Each success rate comes with a 90% Clopper–Pearson interval: with four or five
problems per level the interval is enormous, and stating it is the only honest way
to give the number.

| level | n | solved | rate | 90% interval | mean cost when solved | cost when failed |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| easy | 4 | 4 | 100% | 47% – 100% | $0.0674 | — |
| medium | 3 | 2 | 67% | 14% – 98% | $0.1150 | $0.6886 |
| hard | 4 | 3 | 75% | 25% – 99% | $0.1822 | $1.2486 |
| **total** | 11 | 9 | 82% | 53% – 97% | $0.1162 | $0.9686 |

**MEASURED** — the four `easy` entries are `test`-category problems, sanity checks
written by the archive's authors: 4 out of 4. The seven `medium` and `hard`
entries are variants of real conjectures, category `research solved`: 5 out of 7.
The number to remember is **5 out of 7**, not 9 out of 11.

**MEASURED** — mean cost of a success $0.1162; mean cost of a failure $0.9686. A
failure costs eight times a success, because a failure consumes the whole
per-problem cap while a success stops as soon as the proof passes.

**MEASURED** — cost of the FIRST iteration, median over 11 problems: $0.0430. Two
of the nine successes arrived on the first iteration. This is the price of a
single shot, and strategy C below is built on it.

**MEASURED** — wall-clock time: 0.45 hours in total, of which 0.21 hours were Lean
running locally (46%). The bottleneck is not the API: it is the verifier.

### Cost per problem solved

Formula: (cost when solved × p + cost when failed × (1−p)) / p — what it costs on
average to reach ONE success, retrying on different problems.

| level | p | cost per success | with p at the bottom of the interval | at the top |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| easy | 100% | $0.067 | $1.1 | $0.067 |
| medium | 67% | $0.459 | $4.5 | $0.127 |
| hard | 75% | $0.598 | $4.0 | $0.198 |

The `main` counts hold under the default semantics `google.answer = always_true`,
in which `answer(sorry) ↔ P` becomes `True ↔ P` and the problem really is
provable. Until this session the snapshot also declared a second library that
compiled the same files with `postpone`, and the two sets of `.olean` files
overwrote each other: under that semantics the same 94 problems are not attackable
at all. The surplus library has been disabled — see
[the archive's conventions](01-the-archive.md) — and the numbers above are those
of the right semantics.

Two observations that change the strategy. First: **none** of the 1495 problems
marked `research open` has a complete proof in the archive — the archive is
consistent with itself, there are no "accidentally open" problems to harvest.
Second: 1550 problems are marked `research solved` but have no Lean proof in the
archive: the mathematics is known, the formalisation is missing. That is a third
class of target, easier than the open ones and harder than those the calibration
used.

## 4. Projection onto the open problems

The probabilities of success on an **open** problem are not measurable: they are
the three hypotheses below, and every line declares what it rests on. The
arithmetic accounts for the targets being **finite** in number: once a strategy
has exhausted them, extra spending buys nothing this model can evaluate.

### Cost of an attempt (the basis of the arithmetic)

- **A, full attempt** on an open statement: **$0.97** — **MEASURED**, the mean of
  the calibration's two failures ($0.6886 stopped by the 20 iterations, $1.2486
  stopped by the spending cap). On an open problem an attempt almost always ends
  that way.
- **C, single shot** (sieve): **$0.0430** — **MEASURED**, the median cost of the
  first iteration over the 11 calibration problems.
- **B, a search program** written and run: **$0.10** of API per problem —
  **ESTIMATED**, of the order of the measured cost of the easy problems (mean
  $0.0674). The local computation costs no dollars: it costs machine-nights.

### Strategy A — direct Lean proofs of open statements

One attempt per problem, chosen at random among the 1241 verifiable open ones.
Saturation at **$1,202**.

| spend | attempts | optimistic | realistic | pessimistic |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $50 | 52 | 1.0 | 0.155 | 0.010 |
| $100 | 103 | 2.1 | 0.310 | 0.021 |
| $200 | 206 | 4.1 | 0.619 | 0.041 |
| $500 | 516 | 10 | 1.5 | 0.103 |
| $1000 | 1,032 | 21 | 3.1 | 0.206 |
| $5000 | 1,241 *(saturated)* | 25 | 3.7 | 0.248 |

- **optimistic: p = 2.00%** — ESTIMATED. The automatic probe closed none of the 30
  open statements it tried (240 attempts): the measured 90% upper bound is 9.5%. I
  take about a fifth of that ceiling, because the probe tries tactics whereas the
  agent reasons — so it can do better — but 9.5% is the ceiling from a sample of
  30, not an estimate.
- **realistic: p = 0.30%** — ESTIMATED. One success every ~300 attempts: the
  calibration measures 5/7 on variants ALREADY proved in the archive (proofs of
  10–34 lines), but no open problem has a known short proof, by definition of
  open.
- **pessimistic: p = 0.02%** — ESTIMATED. One success in 5000: the archive is
  curated by DeepMind to collect problems on which the experts have stopped.

### Strategy B — hunting counterexamples with local computation

Here the limit is not money: it is the number of problems on which a search makes
sense. **MEASURED**: the selection script found 30 of them suitable for
computation across the whole archive. Of the four whose literature I checked, two
have a reachable frontier (Euclid numbers: no published systematic search;
Selfridge's conjecture: verified only up to k ≈ 29) and two do not (Erdős 366:
verified to 10²²; Goldbach and Legendre: to 4×10¹⁸). Two out of four, 90%
Clopper–Pearson interval 10% – 90%: **ESTIMATED** 50%, so about **15 real
targets**.

API cost to saturate the strategy: 15 × $0.10 = **$1.50**. Machine time: with 8
searches in parallel, about 2–4 nights.

| spend | searches | optimistic | realistic | pessimistic |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $50 | 15 *(saturated)* | 0.750 | 0.150 | 0.015 |
| $100 | 15 *(saturated)* | 0.750 | 0.150 | 0.015 |
| $200 | 15 *(saturated)* | 0.750 | 0.150 | 0.015 |
| $500 | 15 *(saturated)* | 0.750 | 0.150 | 0.015 |
| $1000 | 15 *(saturated)* | 0.750 | 0.150 | 0.015 |
| $5000 | 15 *(saturated)* | 0.750 | 0.150 | 0.015 |

- **optimistic: p = 5.00%** per search — ESTIMATED. A minority of conjectures have
  low verified bounds (for Selfridge's the literature stops at k ≈ 29): on those,
  local computation really does go past the known.
- **realistic: p = 1.00%** per search — ESTIMATED. Measured in this project: 0
  findings in 3 searches and ~2 CPU-hours; the Euclid-number search passed 2.5
  million primes with nothing.
- **pessimistic: p = 0.10%** per search — ESTIMATED. Published bounds are nearly
  always out of reach: for Erdős 366 the verification reaches 10²².

Strategy B is **saturated at under two dollars of API**. The whole spending
column, from $50 to $5000, changes nothing: what is missing is not money but
problems with a reachable frontier. And any finding is, by
[the protocol](04-finding-protocol.md), more likely a faulty formalisation than a
new result.

### Strategy C — mixed: sieve first, then targeted attempts

Give **a single shot** ($0.0430) to as many problems as possible, up to all 1241;
with what is left, buy $0.97 attempts, starting from the problems where the single
shot showed a sensible plan. The first 10% of the attempts get the amplification
(they are the selected ones), the rest count as strategy A.

| spend | sieved | attempts | optimistic | realistic | pessimistic |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $50 | 1,163 | 0 | 5.1 | 0.767 | 0.051 |
| $100 | 1,241 | 48 | 8.3 | 1.1 | 0.064 |
| $200 | 1,241 | 151 | 13 | 1.6 | 0.085 |
| $500 | 1,241 | 461 | 20 | 2.6 | 0.147 |
| $1000 | 1,241 | 977 | 30 | 4.1 | 0.250 |
| $5000 | 1,241 | 1,241 | 35 | 4.9 | 0.303 |

- the single shot captures **22%** of the successes a full attempt would get —
  **MEASURED**: 2 of the 9 calibration successes arrived on the first iteration;
- selected attempts are worth 3× / 2× / 1× a blind attempt — **ESTIMATED**: the
  single shot discards problems on which the model has no plan at all; in the
  calibration both failures had a coherent plan from the first iteration, so the
  signal exists but is imperfect;
- **MEASURED** — covering all 1241 verifiable open problems with a single shot
  each costs **$53**.

### The comparison in one line

| scenario | dollars per success, A | dollars per success, C | advantage of C |
|---|---|---|---|
| optimistic | $48 | $13 | 3.6× |
| realistic | $323 | $111 | 2.9× |
| pessimistic | $4,843 | $2,185 | 2.2× |

The break-even point of strategy C — one shot at all 1241 open problems, plus a
full attempt on the best 10% — costs **$174**. That is the figure to remember: it
is the price of one complete pass over the archive.

## 5. The local computation, measured

Strategy B is paid in machine time rather than dollars, so the number that counts
is speed.

| search | what it looks for | conclusive? | candidates examined | findings |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `euclide_squarefree` | a prime p with p² dividing a Euclid number | conclusive: a single finding would refute the conjecture | 216,815 | **0** |
| `erdos409_sigma` | orbits of n ↦ σ(n)−1 that never hit a prime | produces suspects to examine by hand, not refutations | 199,999 | **0** |
| `erdos396_binomiale` | the least n with descFactorial(n,k+1) dividing centralBinom(n) | gathers evidence: a "for every k there is n" form cannot be refuted by computation | 61 | **0** |

**MEASURED** — the Euclid-number search examined 216,038 primes in 2,581 seconds,
that is **84 candidates per second** on one core, and reached the prime 2,988,497
with nothing found: for each of those primes ALL primorials with smaller factors
were checked, so the check is complete, not partial. With 8 searches in parallel
and an 8-hour night, that is about 2.4 million candidates per search per night
(**ESTIMATED**: measured speed, extrapolated over time).

**MEASURED** — a complete Lean verification costs 32.9 s with the sandbox and the
fingerprint, 25.0 s without. With 4 verifications in parallel that is about 440
verifications per hour: this, not the API, is the limit on how many proofs can be
checked in a day.

**MEASURED** — an automatic probe on 30 decidable open statements (`decide`,
`plausible`, `norm_num`, `simp_arith`, in direct and negated form, 240 attempts in
all): **0** produced anything. 90% Clopper–Pearson interval on the fraction of
open problems that fall on their own: 0.0% – 9.5%. One apparent case —
`Arxiv.«2107.12475».CollatzLike` — was `plausible` writing "Unable to find a
counter-example" and leaving a `sorry`: the file compiled, but proved nothing. The
verdict rule was corrected (`scripts/sonda_lean.py`).

## 6. What cannot be estimated

1. **The probability that an open problem is solvable by this system.** It is the
   number that decides everything, and it is exactly the one I do not have. No
   sample of solved open problems exists on which to measure it: if it did, those
   problems would not be open. The three scenarios in section 4 are my
   hypotheses, not measurements.

2. **Why calibrating on solved problems is optimistic.** A problem with its proof
   in the archive has, by construction, a short proof: the eleven used here run
   from 1 to 34 lines. Whoever wrote the statement already knew it closed, and
   formalised it so that it would. On an open problem there is no guarantee that a
   short proof exists, nor that the statement is phrased in an attackable form.
   The measured 71% (5 of 7) is the rate on a population that contains NO open
   problem.

3. **Memorisation.** I chose problems that entered the archive after the declared
   training cutoff, but the cutoff concerns the archive, not the mathematics:
   Fermat's quadruple and the 17-vertex counterexample have been in the literature
   for decades. How much of the 9 successes is reconstruction and how much is
   recall, I cannot measure.

4. **How much the iteration limit matters.** One of the two failures stopped
   because it ran out of its 20 iterations, not because it could not do it: with
   60 iterations it might have closed. I have not tried, so I do not count it.

5. **The value of a finding.** If a search finds a counterexample,
   [the protocol](04-finding-protocol.md) foresees three outcomes: faulty
   formalisation, already-known result, new candidate. With zero findings so far I
   have no data on how they would divide, and the likeliest case a priori is the
   first.

## 7. Recommendation

**The mixed strategy (C), and below $174 there is no reason to do anything else.**
Three reasons, in order of weight.

1. *The sieve costs almost nothing and covers everything.* A single shot at one
   problem costs $0.0430, **MEASURED**. With **$50** you get 1163 single shots:
   more than the archive's 1241 verifiable open problems. That is, fifty dollars
   tries EVERY open problem in the collection once, and shows where the model has
   a plan and where it does not. No other spending in this project has a
   comparable information-to-price ratio.

2. *Full attempts should be bought afterwards, not before.* An attempt costs
   $0.97, **MEASURED**, and ends unsolved almost every time. Buying one for each
   of the 1241 open problems costs $1,202 and is the worst way to spend it.
   Sieving all of them and attacking the best 124 costs $174 and, in the realistic
   scenario, returns **2.9 times** the successes per dollar of strategy A.

3. *Local computation is free: always saturate it.* Hunting counterexamples
   consumes no API budget, only machine-nights. It should be kept running
   alongside any strategy, because its marginal cost in dollars is zero. But it
   has to be aimed at the few problems where the published bounds are low: where
   the literature has reached 10²², no night of computation changes anything.

**At what level of spending it makes sense to attempt the open problems.**

- **$53** — the complete sieve: one shot at all 1241 verifiable open problems.
  Expected successes 5.4 / 0.813 / 0.054 (optimistic / realistic / pessimistic).
  Worth doing even expecting zero successes: what you buy is the map of where the
  model has a plan.
- **$174** — the complete sieve plus full attempts on the best 10%. Expected
  successes 13 / 1.6 / 0.080. This is the point where, if the realistic scenario
  is right, a success becomes more likely than not. Below it there is no reason to
  do anything else; above it, you are betting on a number nobody knows.
- **$500** — expected successes 20 / 2.6 / 0.147. Worth it only if the $53 sieve
  showed promising targets: spent blindly, it buys attempts on problems where the
  model had no plan.
- **$1000–$5000** — expected successes 30 / 4.1 / 0.250 and 35 / 4.9 / 0.303. Past
  saturation the arithmetic loses meaning: it would buy second and third attempts
  at the same problems, and the model treats them as independent of the first,
  which they are not. I would not recommend it without reading the sieve's data
  first.

Note the width: at every level of spending the three scenarios span two orders of
magnitude. **The uncertainty is not in the arithmetic: it is all in the value of
p**, which section 6 declares unestimable. Anyone giving a single number here is
guessing.

**A recommendation about targets, not only about spending.** The 1550 problems
marked `research solved` but lacking a proof in the archive are an intermediate
class: the mathematics is known, the formalisation is missing. On those the
success rate would be REAL (the outcome can be checked), the result is useful to
the archive, and the risk of spending for nothing is far lower. If the goal is "do
useful mathematical work with this system" rather than "solve an open problem",
that is the route with the best ratio of cost to result — and the calibration in
hand describes it better than it describes the open problems.

That recommendation was taken: see
[formalising known proofs](07-formalising-known-proofs.md).
